What if the military could change our gender?

read it here

On 1st January 2020 Isabel Fall published 'I Sexually Identify as an Attack Helicopter' in Clarkesworld Magazine. Isabel Fall’s story is unfortunately known more for its context than its contents. Just a few days later, it was taken down by the editor for Fall's safety and health after a deluge of attacks in which she was accused of being a neo-nazi and transphobic. This led to her entering a psychiatric unit after suicidal ideation. The dysphoria brought on by comments saying no woman would ever write this way forced Isabel to publicly out herself as trans and subsequently abandon her gender transition.

What if instead of rejecting and vilifying gender non-conformity, the U.S. military manipulated the same ideas to its advantage? The story starts with Barb, a woman once named Seo Ji Hee. The US military has neuromedically reassigned her gender to 'attack helicopter' to make her a better pilot in the war against an AI government.

"Generations of queer activists fought to make gender a selfdetermined choice, and to undo the creeping determinism that said the way it is now is the way it always was and always must be."

This transition is a strange one. It’s not that Barb is a consciousness implanted into the structure of the helicopter — Barb still has a human body, still disembarks the Apache and goes home and sleeps. It’s more that the way they think has been helicopter-ised. The training that used to enforce the construct of womanhood rewired. “I’m comfortable in open spaces but only if there’s terrain to break it up” they say. “I hate conversations I haven’t started.”

The story metaphorises the weaponisation of gender into the weaponisation of sidewinder missiles and 30 millimeter cannons. It trafficks in the language of implicit threats, of rape. An explosive penetrates a school building.

While roles and genders may be manipulatable, they are still rigidly enforced. Barb’s partner, Axis, the gunner of the Apache, is having doubts about the work they do, the violence they commit. These doubts are a critical threat to the mission; Axis is labeled "dysphoric". But within all the ideas of militarised gender and genderised violence, there is the desire, the smothering yearn, for proficiency. Part of the reasoning for the military’s gender reassignment to its weapon platforms, rather than traditional training, is our built-in aptitude for understanding gender as a system.

“Many of you are veterans in the act of gender. You weigh the gaze and disposition of strangers in a subway car and select where to stand, how often to look up, how to accept or reject conversation. Like a frequency-hopping radar, you modulate your attention for the people in your context: do not look too much, lest you seem interested, or alarming. You regulate your yawns, your appetite, your toilet. You do it constantly and without failure. [In the act of gender,] You are aces. What other way could be better? What other neural pathways are so available to constant reprogramming, yet so deeply connected to judgment, behavior, reflex?”

And what if, instead of all that skill being applied to something as maddeningly vague as socially constructed human relationships, it could be redirected to something real, something as sharp and technological and precise as a military death machine?

As you may have guessed, many of the critics admitted to never actually reading the story in the first place. Isabel took a vile online transphobic 'joke' and turned it into what I think of as a wonderful update to the 'woman as a ship' trope.

Isabel Fall has never published another story.
